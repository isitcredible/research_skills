# Independent literature protocol

## Define the propositions first

Translate the supportable story into propositions that sources can actually
test. Examples:

- the exposure existed in the named place by the required date;
- the proposed intermediate mechanism changed in the required direction;
- affected people or institutions responded through the claimed channel;
- the magnitude is plausible relative to documented constraints;
- the comparison group provides the counterfactual the claim assumes.

Do not begin with a list of famous works and retrofit them to the claim.

## Build the specialist corpus

Search, in order:

1. bibliographies and source discussions in authoritative specialist works;
2. library catalogues and scholarly databases;
3. area-studies and specialist journals;
4. national and university repositories;
5. archival finding aids and digitized primary-source collections;
6. reviews that identify historiographic disputes or standard monographs.

Quantitative studies can map the debate, but they do not count as independent
corroboration when they share the target's data lineage, assumptions, or
identification strategy.

## Vet each work

Record:

- bibliographic identity from the document itself;
- author expertise relevant to the proposition;
- work type and publication venue;
- primary sources, archives, or evidence base;
- relevant pages or sections actually read;
- proposition supported, contradicted, qualified, or not addressed;
- independence from the target paper's data and design.

An abstract or review can identify a candidate work; it cannot settle the
proposition.

## Stylized-fact provenance

A motivating anecdote or canonical stylized fact carries a citation chain,
not evidence. For each stylized fact the paper's story leans on — the
"established" regularities listed in the introduction, the motivating
episode, the well-known case — trace the chain to its origin and ask whether
the source at the end contains evidence or only retells. Academic urban
legends are a real failure class: a story heard secondhand can be canonized
through citation (the Yangtze boat-pullers' hired whipper: a mother's tale →
McManus 1975 → Jensen & Meckling → a "famous fable" recruited to illustrate
the theory it was invented to fit). A citation that terminates in a retold
story is not a source; record the finding as *provenance failure* and mark
the proposition UNRESOLVED or unsupported, noting whether the authors could
have known the chain was unevidenced. Citing a story as evidence while
knowing it may be false is a misrepresentation finding, not a lapse of
taste.

## Acquisition block

When a required work is unavailable, log:

- exact citation;
- proposition it must test;
- searches, databases, catalogues, and repositories tried;
- available substitutes and why they are insufficient;
- likely holdings or access route;
- what the requester/operator must supply.

The overall story status remains `INTERIM` if the missing work could materially
change the coherence verdict.

## Quote and page verification

When quoting or attributing a precise proposition:

1. confirm document identity from its contents;
2. locate the passage in the source;
3. verify wording against the page image or native document;
4. cite the printed page or stable section identifier;
5. keep paraphrase scope no broader than the passage.

## Independence test

Classify each source:

- **Independent:** different evidentiary base and method.
- **Partly independent:** some shared sources but substantial independent
  archival or qualitative evidence.
- **Dependent:** same constructed dataset, source lineage, or identification
  design.
- **Unknown:** evidence base not yet established.

Only the first two categories can independently corroborate the story.
