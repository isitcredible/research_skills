# Independent literature protocol

The check applies to every paper. Where corroboration lives depends on the kind
of proposition, so the sources searched vary with it; the rules for reading,
vetting and classifying them do not.

## Define the propositions first

Translate the supportable story into propositions that sources can actually
test. Examples:

- the exposure or treatment existed where and when the claim requires;
- the proposed intermediate mechanism changed in the required direction;
- affected people, firms or institutions responded through the claimed channel;
- the magnitude is plausible relative to documented constraints and to other
  estimates of the same parameter;
- the comparison group provides the counterfactual the claim assumes;
- a regularity the paper takes as established is documented somewhere.

Do not begin with a list of famous works and retrofit them to the claim.

## Build the specialist corpus

For every proposition, search:

1. bibliographies, handbook chapters, survey articles and other source
   discussions in authoritative specialist works;
2. scholarly databases and library catalogues, including forward citations of
   the target paper and of its key sources;
3. specialist journals of the field the proposition belongs to, which need not
   be economics (demography, epidemiology, political science, area studies);
4. reviews that identify standing disputes or the standard works.

Then add each block the proposition calls for.

**An estimated effect or quantitative regularity:**

- meta-analyses and systematic reviews of the same parameter;
- published replications, re-analyses, comments and replies on the target and
  its close relatives;
- working-paper series and preprint servers, for estimates not yet published;
- trial and pre-analysis registries for designed studies, including registered
  studies that never reported;
- other studies of the same population, policy or episode that use a different
  design or dataset.

**What happened in a particular place, period or institution:**

- national and university repositories;
- archival finding aids and digitized primary-source collections;
- standard monographs and area-studies work.

Quantitative studies can map the debate, but they do not count as independent
corroboration when they share the target's data lineage, assumptions, or
identification strategy. A meta-analysis that pools the target, or studies
built on its data, is not independent of it: read its inclusion list. A body of
agreeing published estimates can share the target's publication filter; where
a meta-analysis reports a selection-corrected estimate, that is the comparison,
not its raw mean.

## Vet each work

Record:

- bibliographic identity from the document itself;
- author expertise relevant to the proposition;
- work type and publication venue;
- evidence base: data, sample and design, or primary sources and archives;
- relevant pages or sections actually read;
- proposition supported, contradicted, qualified, or not addressed;
- independence from the target paper's data and design;
- whether the work has itself been replicated, corrected, commented on or
  retracted.

An abstract or review can identify a candidate work; it cannot settle the
proposition.

## Stylized-fact provenance

A motivating anecdote or canonical stylized fact carries a citation chain,
not evidence. For each stylized fact the paper's story leans on — the
"established" regularities listed in the introduction, the motivating
episode, the well-known case, the widely quoted number — trace the chain to
its origin and ask whether the source at the end contains evidence or only
retells. Academic urban legends are a real failure class: a story heard
secondhand can be canonized through citation (the Yangtze boat-pullers' hired
whipper: a mother's tale → McManus 1975 → Jensen & Meckling → a "famous fable"
recruited to illustrate the theory it was invented to fit). A citation that
terminates in a retold story or an unsourced number is not a source; record
the finding as *provenance failure* and mark the proposition UNRESOLVED or
unsupported, noting whether the authors could have known the chain was
unevidenced. Citing a story as evidence while knowing it may be false is a
misrepresentation finding, not a lapse of taste.

## Acquisition block

When a required work is unavailable, log:

- exact citation;
- proposition it must test;
- searches, databases, catalogues, and repositories tried;
- available substitutes and why they are insufficient;
- likely holdings or access route;
- what the requester/operator must supply.

The overall story status remains `INTERIM` if the missing work could materially
change the coherence verdict.

## Quote and page verification

When quoting or attributing a precise proposition:

1. confirm document identity from its contents;
2. locate the passage in the source;
3. verify wording against the page image or native document;
4. cite the printed page or stable section identifier;
5. keep paraphrase scope no broader than the passage.

## Independence test

Classify each source:

- **Independent:** different evidence base and different design — other data
  or sources, and another way of identifying the effect or establishing the
  fact.
- **Partly independent:** shares some data, sample or sources with the target
  but brings substantial evidence of its own — another dataset, population or
  design, or independent archival or qualitative evidence.
- **Dependent:** same constructed dataset, source lineage, sample, or
  identification design, including later work that re-estimates on the
  target's data.
- **Unknown:** evidence base not yet established.

Only the first two categories can independently corroborate the story.
